With AI, something that would have taken me a week starts to look like an afternoon. Something I would have needed help to attempt looks like something I could do myself. I should feel less busy. Instead, I see more things I could get done.

I could build a piece of software, study a subject I have wanted to understand, or research an idea before putting money into it. I used to have good reasons for leaving those things alone. I did not have the skills, the team, or enough uninterrupted time. Those explanations are getting weaker.

Almost every task now seems within reach, at least far enough to make a serious attempt. An idea I once admired from a distance begins to feel like work I am neglecting.

AI saves time on the task in front of me. It also changes which tasks I believe belong in front of me. For someone who already wants to do more, the second effect can easily outrun the first.

## The disappearing excuse

There is relief in being able to say, honestly, that something is beyond your capabilities. You can put it down without deciding how much you want it.

AI takes away some of that relief. If I can get help with the unfamiliar part and build a rough version to test the idea, “I don't know how” becomes a less convincing reason to wait. I have to decide whether I want to make the attempt. That can feel like there is no excuse left.

Money, health, family responsibilities, access, and expertise still constrain what people can do. A credible first attempt is also a long way from a finished result. But more ideas get past the first objection.

Imagine someone who has wanted to start a small publication. Setting up the site, researching the subject, and preparing illustrations might each have required outside help. With AI, that person can attempt all three. Now the publication looks possible, along with a newsletter to accompany it, a tool for readers, and a second edition in another language.

Before publishing the first issue, they have acquired four projects.

Each project brings decisions, revisions, and obligations that were easy to overlook while getting it started.

## What to do with the saved time

I think AI will make differences in initiative and ambition more visible.

Someone whose goal is to deliver exactly what is expected can use AI to finish the same assignment with less effort. The obligation is satisfied. They feel accomplished, and they stop. If they have little appetite to initiate anything beyond the assignment, AI lets them do less while their output remains perfectly adequate.

An ambitious person may finish a report and start investigating a question it exposed. The investigation suggests an experiment that needs a prototype. They keep spending the time they saved before they have experienced it as free time. Their target grows whenever the effort becomes cheaper.

I mean ambition toward the work in question. Someone who finishes early may put extraordinary drive into raising children, studying music, or building a life outside their job. And a workplace that rewards initiative with nothing but extra assignments gives people a reason to keep their targets fixed. You cannot read a person's character from their hours online.

As AI gives people more of the same capabilities, what they choose to attempt may become a larger difference between them.

There is evidence that work is expanding, even if it cannot establish this explanation. In a [2026 survey of 1,000 U.S. knowledge workers](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/new-research-reveals-how-ai-is-making-jobs-bigger), Atlassian found that heavier AI users reported taking on work across more functions and spending more time on oversight. The survey measured reported behavior, not ambition, and does not prove AI caused the expansion. My interpretation is that cheaper execution gives existing drive more room to act.

## Starting more creates more to finish

AI can help with several projects at once while I still have to care about each of them.

In the publication example, someone must judge whether the translated edition says what the original intended. A reader tool needs maintenance. A newsletter needs another issue. All of that takes attention, however little effort it took to get started.

An ambitious person can mistake all this movement for capacity. There are drafts everywhere. Progress is visible. It feels reasonable to take on one more thing because the tools are handling so much of the work.

Then everything comes back for a decision.

The amount of work I consider mine to finish has grown along with the available help. I can be more capable than I was six months ago and feel further behind because I am measuring myself against a much larger set of things I could accomplish.

I don't want to become less ambitious. I want to get better at considering an idea without committing to it. Something can be feasible and still not deserve a place in my week. I need to be able to say “I could do that, and I am choosing not to” as inability makes fewer of those decisions for me.

## America's AI advantage

I think America's lasting AI advantage is cultural.

Having the labs that build the leading models is an enormous advantage today. But my expectation is that the major models will converge toward being great at most of the work people need. [Stanford's 2026 AI Index](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report) reports a sharply narrowed gap between leading U.S. and Chinese models. That does not prove convergence, but I expect businesses to have several excellent options, even as particular models pull ahead.

The part of America's culture I am betting on rewards people for acting on their ambition and building beyond what anyone has asked of them. I expect that to matter more as the tools improve and more people can attempt work that once seemed beyond them.

For the person starting a publication, access to a great model helps them produce the first issue. A culture that takes their project seriously and rewards them if readers find it useful gives them a reason to start and keep going.

Some of the same restlessness that makes it hard to stop working can turn cheaper execution into new businesses and products. If enough people act on it, more ideas get a serious attempt. Success still requires choosing well and finishing something people want.

Ambition belongs to no single country, and America has to keep making room for people from anywhere who want to build. As great models become widely available, I expect more of the advantage to come from what people choose to do with them. That is why I would bet on America, even as I struggle with what that appetite does to my own workday.
