With AI, a week of work starts to look like an afternoon, and things I'd have needed help with look like things I can do myself. I should feel less busy. Instead I see more things I could get done.

AI saves time on the task in front of me. It also changes which tasks I think belong in front of me. For anyone who already wants to do more, the second effect outruns the first.

## The disappearing excuse

It's a relief to be able to say, honestly, that something is beyond you. You can set it down without deciding how much you want it.

AI takes some of that relief away. If I can get help with the unfamiliar parts and build a rough version, "I don't know how" stops being a good reason to wait. What's left is the question of whether I actually want it. Money, health, family, and expertise still limit what anyone can do, and a first attempt is a long way from a finished result. But more ideas survive the first objection.

Take someone who has always wanted to start a small publication. Building the site, researching the subject, and making the illustrations might each have needed outside help. Now they can do all three. And once the publication looks possible, so do a newsletter to go with it, a tool for readers, and an edition in another language. Before the first issue is out, they have four projects.

## Where the saved time goes

I think AI will make differences in ambition more visible.

Someone whose goal is to deliver what's expected can use AI to finish the same assignment with less effort, and stop. Their output stays perfectly adequate.

An ambitious person finishes the report and starts chasing a question it raised. That leads to an experiment, which needs a prototype. They spend the saved time before it ever feels free. Whenever the effort gets cheaper, the target gets bigger.

By ambition I mean ambition toward that particular work. The person who stops early may be pouring the same drive into raising kids, studying music, or a life outside the job. And a workplace that rewards initiative with nothing but more assignments teaches people to keep their targets small. You can't read someone's character from their hours online.

There's some evidence that work is expanding. In a [2026 survey of 1,000 U.S. knowledge workers](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/new-research-reveals-how-ai-is-making-jobs-bigger), Atlassian found that heavier AI users reported taking on work across more functions and spending more time on oversight. It measured reported behavior, not ambition, and it doesn't show AI caused the change. My read is that cheaper execution gives existing drive more room.

## Starting is cheap, finishing isn't

AI can help with several projects at once, but I still have to care about each of them. Someone has to check that the translated edition says what the original meant. The reader tool needs maintenance. The newsletter needs another issue.

With drafts everywhere and visible progress, it's easy to mistake motion for capacity, and taking on one more thing feels reasonable. Then everything comes back needing a decision.

I'm more capable than I was six months ago and I feel further behind, because I'm measuring myself against a much larger set of things I could do. I don't want to be less ambitious. I want to get better at looking at an idea without committing to it, and at saying "I could do that, and I'm choosing not to," now that inability makes fewer of those decisions for me.

## America's advantage

I think America's lasting AI advantage is cultural.

Having the labs that build the leading models matters today. But I expect the major models to converge on being very good at most of what people need. [Stanford's 2026 AI Index](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report) reports a sharply narrowed gap between the leading U.S. and Chinese models. That doesn't prove convergence, but I expect businesses to have several excellent options.

When everyone has similar tools, what people choose to attempt matters more. The part of American culture I'm betting on rewards people for acting on ambition and building past what anyone asked for. A great model helps the would-be publisher produce the first issue. A culture that takes the project seriously, and rewards it if readers find it useful, gives them a reason to start and keep going.

The restlessness that makes it hard to stop working is the same thing that turns cheaper execution into new businesses. Ambition doesn't belong to any one country, and America has to keep making room for people from anywhere who want to build. But that's the bet I'd make, even while I struggle with what it does to my own workday.
