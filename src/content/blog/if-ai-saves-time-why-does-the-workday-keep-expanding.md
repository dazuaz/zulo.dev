The task gets smaller. The list gets longer.

That is the part of working with AI I keep coming back to. Something that would have taken a week starts to look like an afternoon. Something I would have needed help to attempt starts to look like something I could do myself. I should feel less busy. Instead, I see more things I could get done.

A piece of software. A subject I have wanted to understand. An idea that needs research before it deserves money. Each one used to have a reasonable explanation for staying on the list. I did not have the skills, the team, or enough uninterrupted time.

Those explanations are getting weaker.

Almost every task now seems reachable, at least far enough to make a serious attempt. That does something strange to ambition. An idea I once admired from a distance begins to feel like work I am neglecting.

AI saves time on the task in front of me. It also changes which tasks I believe belong in front of me. For someone who already wants to do more, the second effect can easily outrun the first.

## The disappearing excuse

There is a relief in being able to say, honestly, that something is beyond your capabilities. You can put it down without making a judgment about how much you want it.

AI takes away some of that relief. If I can get help understanding the unfamiliar part, build a rough version, and test the central assumption, “I don't know how” becomes a less convincing reason to wait. The question gets more personal: am I going to make the attempt?

That can feel like there is no excuse left.

Of course there are still constraints. Money, health, family responsibilities, access, and expertise do not disappear because a model can draft a plan. A credible first attempt is also a long way from a finished result. But the point where I can begin keeps moving closer. More ideas get past the first objection.

Imagine someone who has wanted to start a small publication. Previously, setting up the site, researching the subject, and preparing illustrations might each have required outside help. With AI, that person can attempt all three. Now the publication looks possible. So does a newsletter to accompany it, a tool for readers, and a second edition in another language.

Before publishing the first issue, they have acquired four projects.

The workday expands partly because the starting line has become so easy to cross. Every new start brings decisions, revisions, and obligations that the initial burst of progress did not show.

## Two different uses of the same gain

I think AI will make differences in initiative and ambition more visible.

For someone whose goal is to deliver exactly what is expected, AI offers a straightforward bargain. Produce the same report, answer the same requests, complete the same assignment, and spend less effort doing it. The obligation is satisfied. They feel accomplished, and they stop.

Someone with little appetite to initiate anything beyond the assignment can become very efficient at staying within it. AI does more; they do less. Their output may remain perfectly adequate.

For an ambitious person, the bargain is harder to contain. The report is finished, but now they can investigate the question it exposed. The investigation suggests an experiment. The experiment needs a prototype. They keep spending the time they saved before they have experienced it as free time.

One person keeps the target fixed and reduces the effort. Another increases the target whenever effort becomes cheaper.

I mean ambition toward the work in question. Someone who finishes early may put extraordinary drive into raising children, studying music, or building a life outside their job. And a workplace that rewards initiative with nothing but extra assignments gives people a reason to keep their targets fixed. You cannot read a person's character from their hours online.

Still, the distinction matters. Give two people similar capabilities, and what they choose to attempt can become the larger difference between them.

There is evidence that work is expanding, even if it cannot establish this explanation. In a [2026 survey of 1,000 U.S. knowledge workers](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/new-research-reveals-how-ai-is-making-jobs-bigger), Atlassian found that heavier AI users reported taking on work across more functions and spending more time on oversight. The survey measured reported behavior, not ambition, and does not prove AI caused the expansion. My interpretation is that cheaper execution gives existing drive more room to act.

## Starting more creates more to finish

The difficulty is that AI can help with several projects at once while I still have to care about each of them.

In the publication example, generating a translated edition creates a new responsibility: someone must judge whether it says what the original intended. A reader tool needs maintenance. A newsletter needs another issue. Starting those things cheaply does not make their ongoing claims on attention equally cheap.

An ambitious person can mistake all this movement for capacity. There are drafts everywhere. Progress is visible. It feels reasonable to take on one more thing because the tools are handling so much of the work.

Then everything comes back for a decision.

This is how I understand the feeling of being overwhelmed by opportunity. The available help has increased, but so has the amount of work I consider mine to finish. I can be more capable than I was six months ago and feel further behind.

The comparison has changed. I am measuring myself against a much larger set of things I could accomplish.

I don't want the answer to be less ambition. I want to separate a possibility from a commitment. An idea becoming feasible is a reason to consider it; it has not yet earned a place in the week. The ability to say “I could do that, and I am choosing not to” matters more when inability stops making the decision for me.

That choice has consequences beyond an individual's calendar. Organizations and countries will also have to decide what to do with a growing supply of capability.

## America's real AI advantage

I think America's lasting AI advantage is cultural.

Having the labs that build the leading models is an enormous advantage today. But my expectation is that the major models will converge toward being great at most of the work people need. [Stanford's 2026 AI Index](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report) reports a sharply narrowed gap between leading U.S. and Chinese models. That does not prove convergence, but I expect businesses to have several excellent options, even as particular models pull ahead.

America's culture seems well suited to that world. The part I am betting on rewards individual initiative, treats ambition as something to act on, and gives people a reason to build beyond what anyone has asked of them. When more things become possible, that culture has an appetite for attempting them.

Think of the person starting a publication. Access to a great model helps them produce the first issue. A culture that encourages them to start, takes their project seriously, and rewards them if readers find it useful gives them a reason to keep going. The technical capability and the willingness to use it reinforce each other.

That is the connection to the expanding workday. Some of the same restlessness that makes it hard to stop working can turn cheaper execution into new businesses, products, and ideas. Across many people, more possibilities get a serious attempt. Success still requires choosing well and finishing something people want.

Ambition belongs to no single country, and America has to keep making room for people from anywhere who want to build. I would bet on it because I think its culture fits what is coming. As great models become widely available, more of the advantage will come from turning that capability into useful work. The restlessness that expands my workday is one reason I think America will do that well.
